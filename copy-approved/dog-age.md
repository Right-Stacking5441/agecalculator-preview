## Body

## A 7-year-old Labrador

The chart figure comes from the size chart on this page, and the DNA-clock formula has its own section.

A 7-year-old Labrador weighing 30 kg is a large dog on the chart, so the calculator shows **50** human years. The DNA-clock formula gives 16 × ln(7) + 31, which is 16 × 1.946 + 31, or about **62**. Times seven gives 49. The two modern methods disagree by 12 years at this age, so treat any dog-to-human figure as a rough guide.

## The size chart

The figures come from the [pet age chart published on the American Animal Hospital Association website](https://www.aaha.org/wp-content/uploads/globalassets/02-guidelines/canine-life-stage-2019/canine_and_feline_age_chart_poster.pdf). The chart is a Zoetis poster, and its dog figures are adapted from the Dog Owner's Home Veterinary Handbook. Every dog counts as 15 at one year old and 24 at two. After that the sizes split. The weight bands below follow the poster's pounds and kilograms, with the joins closed so every adult weight sits in a band:

| Dog's age | Small, up to 9.5 kg (21 lb) | Medium, 9.5 to 23.1 kg (21 to 51 lb) | Large, 23.1 to 40.8 kg (51 to 90 lb) | Giant, over 40.8 kg (over 90 lb) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 28 | 28 | 30 | 32 |
| 5 | 36 | 37 | 40 | 42 |
| 7 | 44 | 47 | 50 | 56 |
| 8 | 48 | 51 | 55 | 64 |
| 10 | 56 | 60 | 66 | 78 |
| 12 | 64 | 69 | 77 | 93 |
| 15 | 76 | 83 | 93 | 115 |

Small dogs add 4 human years for each year after two. Medium dogs add 4 or 5 a year, and large dogs 5 or 6. Giant breeds add 7 or 8 a year from about age six.

The calculator gives the chart figure for whole years and reads in a straight line between two printed ages for anything in between. The chart prints whole years only. Reading in a straight line between them is this site's own method, not the chart's. So a large dog of 7 years and 6 months comes out at about 53 (52.5 rounded), between 50 at 7 and 55 at 8. Dogs under 1 year aren't shown, because the chart starts at 1. Giant dogs stop at 16 years and the other sizes stop at 20, because that is where the chart ends. The calculator doesn't make up figures beyond it.

Pick the size by the weight your dog is, or will be, as an adult. As a rough guide, a Border Collie is medium or large (14 to 25 kg, or 30 to 55 lb, according to the [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/dog-breeds/border-collie/)), a Labrador large and a Great Dane giant. Crossbreeds go by adult weight. The calculator asks you to choose a size rather than type a weight. If your dog's adult weight sits on a join, for example 9.5 kg, pick either neighbouring size; the poster's own printed bands had a small gap there.

## Reading the result

Give your dog's age in years and months and pick their adult size. The main figure, from a size-based chart, is an estimate of their age in human years. Under More you'll see a second estimate from a 2020 DNA study, and the old "times seven" figure, so you can see how far apart the methods are.

Every figure here is an estimate. Breed, genetics and health all change how a particular dog ages. For anything about your dog's health, your vet is the person to ask.

## Why not just multiply by seven?

Dogs grow up far faster than people at the start and then slow down, and large dogs age faster than small ones later in life, which is why this dog age calculator asks for their size. On the size chart above, a one-year-old dog of any size already counts as 15 in human years, not 7. The American Veterinary Medical Association says that [dogs do not age at a rate of 7 human years per dog year](https://www.avma.org/resources/pet-owners/petcare/senior-pets).

## The DNA-clock estimate (Wang et al. 2020)

In 2020 a team led by Trey Ideker at UC San Diego published a study in Cell Systems comparing age-related DNA methylation in [104 dogs, mostly Labrador retrievers, with 320 people](https://www.cell.com/cell-systems/fulltext/S2405-4712%2820%2930203-9). They found the relationship is logarithmic, and fitted the formula:

human age = 16 × ln(dog age) + 31

where ln is the natural logarithm. By this measure a one-year-old dog is about 31 and a four-year-old about 53, much older than the chart suggests for young dogs. [The study itself](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7484147/) notes the limit: it focused on Labrador retrievers, and breeds with very different lifespans "could yield different age-translation functions". The [NIH release](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-researchers-reframe-dog-human-aging-comparisons) says the same, though [UC San Diego's](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/how-old-is-your-dog-in-human-years-scientists-develop-better-method-than-multiply-by-7) lead researcher expects the clock to apply to all breeds. It also doesn't take size into account. The calculator shows it as a second opinion, not the main result. Like the chart figure, it isn't shown for dogs under one year. The study did compare puppies with babies (it matched about 8 weeks in dogs to about 9 months in humans), but at that age the formula is very sensitive to rounding, and below about seven and a half weeks it falls to zero or below, so this page keeps to adult dogs.

## When is a dog a senior?

Larger dogs have shorter average lifespans and reach their senior years sooner, which is why the chart climbs faster for them after age two. The [AVMA's senior pets guidance](https://www.avma.org/resources/pet-owners/petcare/senior-pets) gives these rough ages: small breeds 8 to 11 years, medium 8 to 10, large 8 to 9, and giant breeds 6 to 7. It recommends senior pets see a vet at least twice a year.

For cats, the curve is different and size matters much less. The [cat age calculator](/cat-age) covers it.

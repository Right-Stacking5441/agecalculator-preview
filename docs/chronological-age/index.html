How months are counted

A completed month lands on the same date next month. If that date doesn’t exist (31 January to 1 March, or a 29 February birthday in a common year), it rolls to the 1st of the next month. Some software, including common date libraries, uses the last day of the month instead, so results can differ by a day around month ends.

Filling in the score sheet

Put in the date of birth and the test date, and the child’s age on that day comes out in the years;months;days form score sheets use, for example 8;4;12. It is meant for teachers, SENCOs, educational psychologists, and speech and language therapists filling in standardised test forms. The result also shows the age in total months, a months figure with 15 or more days rounded up, and buttons to copy the code or print a one-line slip for the file.

This chronological age calculator counts the time between two dates. It isn’t an official tool from any test publisher. If your test manual has its own rule for days or rounding, the manual decides.

Reading a chronological age result

8;4;12 means 8 years, 4 months and 12 days on the test date. Some forms write it 8:4 or 8-4 and drop the days; copy whatever your form asks for. The “months” line is the same age as one number (8;4;12 is 100 months and 12 days), which some norms tables use instead.

The “borrow 30 days” method, and why the days can differ

When the test day is smaller than the birth day, paper methods borrow a month from the month column and add 30 days to the day column. When the test month is smaller than the birth month, they borrow a year and add 12 months.

Take a child born on 15 January 2019 and tested on 10 March 2026. On paper: 10 is less than 15, so borrow, giving 40 minus 15 = 25 days, and the test month drops from 3 to 2. Then 2 minus 1 = 1 month, and 2026 minus 2019 = 7 years. That gives 7;1;25.

This calculator counts on the real calendar instead. 15 January 2026 is the 7th birthday, 15 February is one month on, and from 15 February to 10 March is 23 days, because February 2026 had 28 days, not 30. So the result here is 7;1;23. Borrowing 30 days treats every month as 30 days long, so whenever the test day is earlier in the month than the birth day, the days can differ by up to two: one after a 31-day month, two after a 28-day February. Years and months always match. If your manual tells you to borrow 30 days, use its answer for the form.

Why not just subtract the years?

At young ages, norms tables move every few months. Two children born on 14 and 19 November 2020 and both tested on 16 November 2026 are 6;0;2 and 5;11;28, and they may well be scored on different rows. Subtracting the birth year from the test year makes them both 6, which would put them on the same row.

Leap days

A child born on 29 February has their birthday counted on 1 March in common years (why). If the test manual gives its own instruction, follow the manual. On this rule, a child born on 29 February 2020 is 6;8;18 on 16 November 2026.

If you’re scoring a test

Pearson’s WIAT-III guidance

Pearson’s WIAT-III guidance says “all months are assumed to have 30 days”, so for that test the days figure can differ from this calculator’s by up to two. The same guidance says ages are not rounded up. Some other manuals round 15 or more days up to the next month, which is why the result shows that figure separately.

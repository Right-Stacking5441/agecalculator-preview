A completed month lands on the same date next month. If that date doesn’t exist — 31 January to 1 March, or a 29 February birthday in a common year — it rolls to the 1st of the next month. Some software, including common date libraries, uses the last day of the month instead, so results can differ by a day around month ends.

Reading the result

8;4;12 means 8 years, 4 months and 12 days on the test date. A child born on 4 July 2018 and tested on 16 November 2026 is 8;4;12. Some forms write it 8-4 or 8:4 and leave the days off; copy whichever your form asks for.

This is a date-difference helper. It isn’t an official tool from Pearson, Yale or any test publisher, and some manuals have their own rule for days, such as rounding 15 or more days up to the next month. The result shows that rounded figure separately so you can use it if your manual asks for it.

Why not just subtract the years?

Because norms tables change every few months at young ages, a child who is 5;11;28 is scored against a different row from one who is 6;0;2. Subtracting birth year from test year would put them in the same row. The calculator borrows months and days properly, including across short months and leap years.

Questions people ask

Is this the same method the Pearson or Yale calculators use?

It gives the same completed years, months and days for ordinary dates. Around the end of a month, calculators that “borrow” 30 days can be a day different. If your manual says how to borrow, follow it.

Do you store the names I type?

No. The page has no server behind the form. Close the tab and they’re gone.

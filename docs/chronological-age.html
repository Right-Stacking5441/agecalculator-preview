What this number is for

        
Teachers, speech and language therapists, and psychologists often have to write a chronological age on a test form as years, months and days, for example 8;4;12. That is not a different kind of age from the homepage. It is the same civil count, labelled with a test date and printed in the compact style the forms expect.

        
The arithmetic is completed years, leftover months, leftover days, with 1 March as the anniversary of a 29 February birthday in a common year, and with 31 January plus one month falling on the last day of February. A child born on 15 March 2018, tested on 16 November 2026, has a chronological age of 8;8;1.

        
This is not an official Pearson, Yale, Gesell, or Super Duper tool, and it does not apply a publisher’s rounding rule. If the manual on your desk says to drop leftover days, or to count a month differently, follow the manual. The copy button and a print of this page give you a short slip: date of birth, test date, and the y;m;d figure.

      

    

      
Questions people actually ask

      

        
What does 8;4;12 mean?

        
Eight completed years, four completed months, and twelve leftover days on the test date. It is the same civil count as the homepage, written in the compact form used on many school and SLP forms.

      

      

        
Is this an official Pearson or Yale calculator?

        
No. It is a date-difference helper. A test manual may use its own rounding rule. Check the manual you are scoring against before you write the figure on a form.

A completed month lands on the same date next month. If that date doesn’t exist (31 January to 1 March, or a 29 February birthday in a common year), it rolls to the 1st of the next month. Some software, including common date libraries, uses the last day of the month instead, so results can differ by a day around month ends.

This chronological age calculator takes a date of birth and a test date and gives the child’s age on that day in the years;months;days form used on score sheets, for example 8;4;12. It is meant for teachers, SENCOs, educational psychologists, and speech and language therapists filling in standardised test forms. The result also shows the age in total months, a months figure with 15 or more days rounded up, and buttons to copy the code or print a one-line slip for the file.

It is a date-difference helper. It isn’t an official tool from any test publisher. If your test manual has its own rule for days or rounding, the manual decides.

Reading a chronological age result

8;4;12 means 8 years, 4 months and 12 days on the test date. Some forms write it 8:4 or 8-4 and drop the days; copy whatever your form asks for. The “months” line is the same age as one number (8;4;12 is 100 months and 12 days), which some norms tables use instead.

A worked example

A child born on 4 July 2018 is tested on 16 November 2026. Set out the way test manuals do it, test date on top:

YearMonthDay
Test date20261116
Date of birth201874
Chronological age8412

No borrowing is needed here, so the answer is 8;4;12, or 100 months. With 12 days left over, a manual that rounds 15 or more days up would still say 100 months.

The “borrow 30 days” method, and why the days can differ

When the test day is smaller than the birth day, paper methods borrow a month from the month column and add 30 days to the day column. When the test month is smaller than the birth month, they borrow a year and add 12 months.

Take a child born on 15 January 2019 and tested on 10 March 2026. On paper: 10 is less than 15, so borrow, giving 40 minus 15 = 25 days, and the test month drops from 3 to 2. Then 2 minus 1 = 1 month, and 2026 minus 2019 = 7 years. That gives 7;1;25.

This calculator counts on the real calendar instead. 15 January 2026 is the 7th birthday, 15 February is one month on, and from 15 February to 10 March is 23 days, because February 2026 had 28 days, not 30. So the result here is 7;1;23. Borrowing 30 days treats every month as 30 days long, so whenever the test day is earlier in the month than the birth day, the days can differ by up to two: one after a 31-day month, two after a 28-day February. Years and months always match. If your manual tells you to borrow 30 days, use its answer for the form.

Why not just subtract the years?

At young ages, norms tables move every few months. Two children born on 14 and 19 November 2020 and both tested on 16 November 2026 are 6;0;2 and 5;11;28, and they may well be scored on different rows. Subtracting the birth year from the test year makes them both 6. Months and days matter.

Leap days

This calculator counts a 29 February birthday as falling on 1 March in common years. That is the rule in Scottish law under the Age of Legal Capacity (Scotland) Act 1991, section 6(2); the law in England, Wales and Northern Ireland does not name a date, and a test manual may have its own instruction. So a child born on 29 February 2020 is 6;8;18 on 16 November 2026. For everyday ages and birthdays, the main age calculator uses the same rule.

Questions people ask

What is chronological age on a test?

The child’s exact age on the day of testing, written as years, months and days, for example 8;4;12. Standardised tests use it to pick the right row in the norms tables.

How do you calculate chronological age by hand?

Write the test date above the date of birth as year, month, day and subtract each column from the right. If the days are short, borrow a month and add 30 days; if the months are short, borrow a year and add 12 months.

Does it match the 30-day method in test manuals?

Years and months match. Days can differ by up to two whenever the test day is earlier in the month than the birth day, because this calculator uses real month lengths and the manual method assumes 30 days. Pearson’s WIAT-III guidance, for example, says “all months are assumed to have 30 days.”

Should I round up the days?

Only if your test manual says to. Pearson’s WIAT-III guidance says ages are not rounded up. Some other manuals round 15 or more days up to the next month; the result shows that figure separately so you can use it if needed.

Are the names I type stored?

No. They stay in your browser tab and disappear when you close it.
